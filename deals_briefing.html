Message spine

Four things. Everything routes back to one.
01

A debut is an eligibility event, not a funding event

No liquidity book buys a first deal on its merits. They buy the expectation of a curve, a repeat calendar and index inclusion. Build for the tenth deal.

02

The plumbing is the price

Liquid-asset treatment, risk weight, repo and index eligibility are a chain, not a menu. The weakest link sets the clearing spread — usually by more than extra overcollateralisation would.

03

Liquidity is a promise, not a feature

A new or small programme gets bought on the assumption it can be sold. Consistent size, a committed secondary and predictable timing beat most structural enhancements.

04

Diversification has a price, and it is payable

Investors will pay to learn a new name — in analyst time, not just spread. The issuer pays in transparency, consistency and access. That trade is available to anyone willing to make it.

"I don't buy the debut. I buy the curve the debut promises."

"Every new jurisdiction writes a beautiful law and then forgets the buyer's rulebook."

"The first deal is marketing. The third deal is a market."

"I can price credit risk. I can't price surprise — which is why reporting beats structure."

"Eighty deals is the easy part. Eighty issuers who come back next year is the hard part."

A. The opener Kemmish goes round the table fast — have 25 seconds ready

01
EasyInvestor lens

Why covered bonds — and why now?

Buyer's inversion of the panel title. The constraint on a bank liquidity portfolio in 2026 isn't yield, it's eligible supply. Covered bonds are one of very few asset classes that are simultaneously high-quality liquid, low risk weight, repo-eligible and genuinely diversifiable across ~30 jurisdictions. Every new market is one more line I'm allowed to own. Hook: "which is why I care more about who issues next year than who issued last week."

02
EasyInvestor lens

What does Markets Treasury actually do, and why should a debut issuer in this room care about you?

Describe the function generically: investing a bank's commercial surplus in high-quality liquid assets across currencies, held through a cycle rather than traded. Why it matters to them: this buyer type is large, repeatable and rules-driven — the closest thing to a structural bid a new programme can build toward. No sizes, no numbers, no book detail.

03
EasyCross-border

Has the investable universe genuinely widened in five years, or does it just feel that way at conferences?

Both — separate them. Genuinely wider: CEE supply grew roughly 94% year-on-year in H1 2026, harmonisation gave a common vocabulary, non-EEA frameworks matured. Feels wider: much new-jurisdiction paper still isn't eligible in the ways that turn interest into ownership. "The map has more countries on it than my portfolio does, and closing that gap is what this panel is actually about."

04
MediumInvestor lensReputational risk

What's on your "would like to, but can't yet" list?

Trap: naming countries you won't buy makes a headline and an angry email. Answer in criteria, never names.

"I'd rather give you the test than the list." Four gates: a bankruptcy-remote framework that has been tested or at least stress-argued; a rating outcome that survives the sovereign; an eligibility profile a bank book can use; a plausible repeat calendar. "Any market that clears those is on my list. Any market that doesn't, I can admire but not own."

05
MediumInvestor lens

Give me one thing this market gets wrong about investors.

That the negotiation is about spread. For a first-time name the binding constraint is usually onboarding cost — credit lines, country limits, legal review, ops set-up, index treatment. Fixed cost against an uncertain calendar. Issuers can lower it far more cheaply than they can pay for it in bp.

B. The investor lens Home ground — most of your airtime lives here

06
EasyInvestor lens

What do you look at first on a debut covered bond from a new jurisdiction?

Give an ordered list — ordered lists survive editing into a write-up. (1) Can I hold it: liquid-asset classification, risk weight, repo and index eligibility. (2) What happens on issuer default: segregation, administrator powers, liquidity buffer, extendable maturity. (3) Rating linkage to sovereign and issuer. (4) Pool data quality and reporting cadence. (5) Who else is in the book — a book of one is not a market.

07
MediumInvestor lens

Rank them: legal framework, sovereign rating, issuer credit, secondary liquidity, index inclusion.

Refuse the ranking, give a better structure: they aren't ranked, they're sequential gates. Framework and eligibility are binary. Sovereign and issuer set the range. Liquidity and index set where you sit inside it. "Most debut discussions spend 80% of the time on the third question and 20% on the first."

08
HardInvestor lensReputational risk

How much extra does a new name have to pay? Give me a number.

Hard no on a number. Any figure becomes an HSBC price-talk quote in someone's write-up.

"I'll give you the shape rather than the number, because the number depends on things neither of us knows yet." The new-jurisdiction premium is mostly a liquidity and operational premium, not a credit premium — which is why it decays with the second and third transaction rather than with a rating upgrade. Public anchor if you want one: non-EEA covered bonds have persistently traded wider than EEA paper, and published estimates of the compression from an equivalence regime have been mid-single-digit bp. Attribute to research, never to your book.

09
HardInvestor lensReputational risk

Would you buy Uzbekistan? — asked with the CEO of UzMRC beside you

The single most dangerous question on this panel. "Yes" is a commitment you can't make. "No" is a headline and a discourtesy. Answer the class, not the country.

"Whether I can buy something and whether it's good are different questions, and only the first is mine to answer on a stage." Then: my constraint set is entirely public — liquid-asset classification, risk weight, repo eligibility, index. Any market that solves those gets a look; the ones that don't, I follow rather than own. Close generously and specifically: "and the interesting thing about the mortgage refinancing company model — Uzbekistan, Korea, Malaysia, Japan — is that it solves the aggregation problem before the funding problem, which is the right order." Warm, useful, non-committal.

10
MediumInvestor lens

What kills a deal for you after you've already said yes in principle?

Three killers, in order: a late change to structure or documentation; a reporting gap against what was promised; and a size too small to be index-eligible once the book shrinks. "None of those are credit events. All of them are surprise, and surprise is the one thing I genuinely can't price."

11
MediumInvestor lensStructure & reg

Is a covered bond from a frontier market really a covered bond, or a secured bank bond in a costume?

Kemmish-bait — enjoy it, then be rigorous. The label earns its keep if three things hold: assets genuinely segregated on issuer insolvency; someone with authority and liquidity to keep paying after default; investor keeps dual recourse. If they hold, the name is earned regardless of geography. If not, using the label damages the asset class more than it helps the issuer — "and that's the market's problem to police, not the investor's."

12
MediumInvestor lens

Can a covered bond meaningfully out-rate its sovereign — and do you believe it when it does?

Methodologies allow uplift within limits; that's the agencies' territory, defer to it. The buyer's version: uplift is worth something only if the reason for it survives the scenario that stressed the sovereign in the first place. Currency, payment systems and an administrator's practical ability to operate during sovereign stress all sit outside the rating. "I underwrite the mechanism, not the notch."

13
EasyInvestor lensDebut mechanics

What's a minimum viable programme — size, tenor, frequency — before you'd onboard the name?

Frame in the market's public conventions, not your limits: benchmark size thresholds and rating floors are index rules anyone can read; tenor should sit where the curve already has liquidity, not where the issuer's ALM would prefer; frequency matters most — "one deal a year that always happens beats two that sometimes do." Say explicitly you're describing convention, not an internal threshold.

14
MediumInvestor lens

Does harmonised transparency reporting change anything, or is it compliance theatre?

Be specific to be credible: the value isn't that anyone reads every field, it's that a new name becomes comparable without a bespoke project. It converts a research question into a screening question — exactly the onboarding cost from Q05. "Standardisation doesn't win you the trade. It gets you into the comparison, and you can't win a comparison you're not in."

15
HardInvestor lensReputational risk

Be honest — do investors do credit work on covered bonds, or just buy the label and the risk weight?

Designed to make you defensive or indiscreet. Be candid about the market, precise about your own discipline, and don't disparage other investors.

Concede the true part first — disarming, and it's what he wants. A large share of demand here is rules-driven by construction; that's the point of a regulated liquid asset and why spreads are stable. Then pivot: "which is exactly why the marginal buyer matters. When rules-driven demand is satisfied, the price is set by whoever did the work — and in a stress, that's the only bid on the screen."

16
MediumInvestor lens

What would actually make you sell?

Categories, not positions: a change in eligibility (classification, risk weight, index exclusion) does more selling than a change in credit; a loss of reporting; a structural amendment you didn't underwrite. "Credit deterioration is usually the slowest of the reasons, which tells you something about what this asset class is really exposed to."

17
MediumInvestor lens

Cover pool or issuer — which do you actually underwrite?

"I underwrite the issuer, and I stress the pool." In practice covered bonds are bought as a bank exposure with a very good tail hedge; the pool determines the shape of the tail, not the probability of getting there. It's the honest description, and it explains why sovereign and bank fundamentals still drive spreads in a supposedly ring-fenced product.

18
HardInvestor lensReputational risk

Are bank treasuries distorting this market by being price-insensitive buyers?

Correct the premise precisely rather than rejecting it. Bank liquidity books are rules-sensitive rather than price-insensitive — they buy what regulation says is liquid, in the currencies the balance sheet needs, and are relatively indifferent to 2bp because they're solving a different problem. That does compress dispersion in normal times. The interesting question is what happens when the rules change rather than the price. "If you want to know what my bid does next, read the regulation, not the screen."

C. Structure & regulation Overlaps Roundtable 2 and Panel V — tee them up, don't duplicate

19
MediumStructure & regCross-border

The EBA has recommended third-country equivalence built on reciprocity. Good news, or a decade of process?

Get the public facts right: the EBA's September 2025 advice supported an equivalence regime in principle, with reciprocity as a condition, and commentators have flagged that implementation is the hard part. Your angle: equivalence changes the eligibility of non-EEA paper for EU bank buyers, and eligibility gates matter more than spread. But reciprocity is a political mechanism applied to a technical problem — "it will help the markets that already had the option, and take longest for the markets that need it most."

20
MediumStructure & reg

Would equivalence change what you buy, or just what you pay?

Both, in that order. It first changes the size of the eligible universe for regulated European buyers; spread convergence is the consequence, not the cause. Published estimates of the compression have been mid-single-digit bp — cite as research, not as your target.

21
HardStructure & regReputational risk

Is reciprocity fair on small markets with no EU issuers to reciprocate for?

Policy fairness is political. Answer on consequences, and don't criticise a named regulator.

"I'll leave fairness to the people who drafted it and stick to what it does." A reciprocity test rewards two-way flow; markets whose banks fund domestically have nothing to trade, so the test bites hardest exactly where market-building is most needed. That's an argument for outcome-based assessment alongside reciprocity — a design question already being discussed publicly. Hand to Rada or to Panel V.

22
MediumStructure & regInvestor lens

Liquidity classification, risk weight, repo eligibility, index inclusion — which matters most?

Your cleanest set-piece and the clearest version of Message 02. They're a chain, not a menu: the weakest link sets the clearing price because each removes a different buyer type. Lose liquid-asset classification and you lose bank treasuries; lose the risk weight and you lose capital-constrained buyers; lose repo and you lose the leveraged bid; lose the index and you lose the passive bid. "Adding overcollateralisation to fix a missing eligibility is like adding a fifth lock to a door that isn't in a wall."

23
MediumStructure & reg

Name one regulatory change that would open the most new markets.

Have one ready — vagueness wastes the slot. Best candidate: a predictable, outcome-based route to eligibility for non-EEA frameworks, with a published timetable. Not because the treatment is generous, but because a known process lets an issuer build a five-year plan. Today many can't tell whether the work pays off, so they don't start.

24
MediumStructure & reg

Securitisation reform is making RMBS cheaper to hold. Does the covered bond moat narrow?

Concede the direction, dispute the substitution. They compete for the issuer's collateral, not the investor's mandate — a liquidity portfolio and an ABS mandate are different books with different rules. What reform genuinely changes is the issuer's marginal choice of where to put a mortgage, which changes covered supply and pool composition later. "The competition happens on the origination side and shows up in my market two years later."

25
MediumStructure & reg

Has harmonisation made covered bonds boring in a good way, or squeezed out the innovation new markets need?

A genuine two-hander — good place to differ politely with Elena. Harmonisation lowered the cost of comparison (good for new entrants) but raised the entry bar and made "we're nearly compliant" a weak pitch. Net: helps issuers who can meet the standard, hurts those needing a transitional path. "Standards are a ladder for some and a wall for others; the design question is whether we build any rungs."

26
HardStructure & regReputational risk

Should the EU insist others copy EU legislation, or accept outcome equivalence?

Don't tell a regulator what to do from an FT stage while wearing a bank's name. Frame as a trade-off with consequences.

"Both approaches have a cost, and it's worth being explicit about who pays it." Copy-in gives certainty and excludes functioning frameworks built on other legal traditions — common law, contractual structures, agency models. Outcome-based is more inclusive and much more expensive to supervise. Then the neutral buyer's view: "I need predictability more than I need either. Tell me the test and I'll tell my issuers how to pass it."

27
MediumStructure & reg

Soft bullet, conditional pass-through — do extendable maturities help a new market or scare investors?

Soft bullet is close to market standard and priced as such. Conditional pass-through solves a rating problem and creates an investor problem: extension risk is hard to hold in a portfolio measured on liquidity, so it can narrow the very buyer base a new issuer needs. "It's a legitimate tool for getting a rating. It is rarely a good tool for getting a book." This is your polite disagreement with the structurer's instinct.

D. From concept to debut The core of the session's stated agenda

28
EasyDebut mechanics

What should a first-time issuer do twelve months out that they usually don't?

Three concrete actions: publish pool data before you need to — a year of reporting nobody reads is the cheapest credibility you will ever buy; get the eligibility questions answered in writing before you write the prospectus; and meet investors who will say no, because their objections are your document's to-do list. "Roadshow the people who can't buy you yet. They're the ones who'll tell you why."

29
EasyDebut mechanics

What makes a good roadshow from the investor's chair?

Bring the person who can answer the follow-up — a treasurer without a lawyer or risk officer stalls on the third question. Lead with framework and eligibility, not the country's GDP growth. Say what you'll do next year, not just this week. Answer "what goes wrong" before you're asked.

30
MediumDebut mechanicsReputational risk

How much does an anchor investor — EBRD, IFC, a development bank — move your decision?

Rada Tomova is beside you. The honest answer is genuinely complimentary if framed right.

"An anchor buys time, not conviction — and time is the scarcer of the two." What a DFI anchor really delivers: diligence you can partly rely on, a completed first transaction so a curve can start, and a signal that someone with country expertise stayed in the room. What it can't do is make the bond eligible or liquid. "I'd rather a development bank spent a euro on the framework than on the order book — and in fairness, that's mostly what EBRD does."

31
MediumDebut mechanicsCross-border

Local currency first, or hard currency first?

Strong, well-supported view: local first, almost always. The covered bond's job in a developing market is to build a domestic long-term funding curve; a hard-currency debut that isn't naturally hedged imports FX risk into a mortgage book to solve a funding problem. Exception: when the domestic investor base genuinely doesn't exist yet and international issuance is a way of importing standards. Nod to EBRD's local-currency work — accurate and generous.

32
MediumDebut mechanicsCross-border

EUR, USD or GBP for an international debut — how should an issuer choose?

Your most distinctive contribution, because you hold all three. Currency is a choice of rulebook and buyer, not of cost: EUR gives the deepest, most rules-driven bid and clearest index architecture; USD reaches a bigger, more ratings-led buyer with larger tickets and 144A machinery; GBP is smaller but stickier, floating-rate in habit, and a genuine home for issuers with natural sterling assets. "Pick the currency whose investors you want still there in year five, then solve the basis."

33
MediumDebut mechanics

Is a domestic market a prerequisite for an international one, or a distraction?

Prerequisite for durability, not for the first trade. A domestic bid stops an international programme becoming a fair-weather product — it's the buyer of last resort when cross-border investors step back. "Every market that only ever issued abroad has eventually discovered why that was a problem."

34
EasyDebut mechanics

Most common mistake in a debut investor presentation?

Selling the country instead of the structure — thirty slides of macro and four on the cover pool. The investor's question isn't "is this a good economy", it's "what do I own on the day the issuer stops paying". Runner-up: no page on eligibility.

35
MediumDebut mechanics

How much does the third deal matter compared with the first?

Your line-of-the-day candidate: "the first deal is marketing, the third deal is a market." Three points make a curve; a curve makes relative value; relative value makes a secondary; a secondary makes a buyer who wasn't in the first book. "Which is why I judge a debut by the calendar behind it, not the cover of it."

36
MediumDebut mechanics

Harder sell: a small issuer in a big market, or a big issuer in a small market?

Made for this panel — Triodos and Fairstone are the first, UzMRC arguably the second. Small-in-big has the framework and comparables but competes with national champions for attention and pays a size and liquidity premium. Big-in-small has scarcity value and national-champion status but carries country and framework risk. "The small issuer's problem is being ignored. The small market's problem is being unowned. Only one of those can be fixed by the issuer alone."

37
MediumDebut mechanics

Is the mortgage refinancing agency model — Uzbekistan, Korea, Malaysia, Japan, Canada Mortgage Bonds — a shortcut or a crutch?

Even-handed and useful, and it lets you be generous to Farmanov on substance. It solves aggregation and standardisation, genuinely the hard part in a fragmented banking system, and creates one credit investors can learn instead of twenty. The risk is that it substitutes for bank-level capability so nothing develops behind it, and concentrates a market in a single quasi-public issuer. "It's the right first institution. The test is whether it makes itself less necessary over a decade."

38
HardDebut mechanicsReputational risk

Should development banks be creating markets that wouldn't otherwise exist?

A question about someone else's mandate, asked in front of them. Don't adjudicate — reframe as design.

"Not my mandate to judge, but there's a version of the question I can answer." Market-building works when spent on framework, data and standards, because those persist. It works less well spent on price, because the first deal needing a subsidy usually means the second will too. "Build the road, don't pay for the taxi."

E. ESG and green Triodos owns this ground — contribute mechanics, not doctrine

39
MediumESGInvestor lens

Does a green label change your bid?

Don't quantify a greenium and don't attribute a pricing view to HSBC.

Separate the two effects — the sophisticated answer. A label reliably widens the buyer list (mandated funds that otherwise couldn't participate) and only sometimes narrows the spread. For an established issuer the second effect is what gets discussed. For a debut, the first is worth far more: more accounts means a better-distributed book, which is the durable benefit. "Green is a distribution tool before it's a pricing tool, and debut issuers should value it that way."

40
MediumESGStructure & reg

The EuGB standard: help or hurdle for a new market?

Both — be concrete about which. It raises credibility and cuts the "is it really green" diligence. It also demands taxonomy alignment and housing-stock data many markets don't have. "For a new market the honest sequence is a credible use-of-proceeds framework now and taxonomy alignment when the data exists — not a EuGB label the pool can't support." Cross-reference: Workshop C at this congress covers 2026 green maturities and the refinance-green-or-go-conventional trade-off.

41
HardESGReputational risk

Isn't ESG a rich-market luxury? Frontier markets just need housing finance.

Politically loaded in every direction. Do not take a side on the politics.

Move it from values to credit, where you're solid and additive: the energy performance and climate exposure of a housing stock is a collateral quality question — it affects running costs, affordability, insurability and long-run value, at any income level. "I'm not asking a new issuer for a label. I'm asking what their pool looks like in thirty years, which is the same question I ask everyone." Then hand to Kees, whose numbers make the point for you: 63% of homes Triodos finances carry an energy label of A or above.

42
MediumESG

Would you buy a green covered bond from a jurisdiction with no taxonomy?

Answer with the test, not the verdict: what matters is whether criteria are published, externally reviewed and reported against consistently. A local standard applied honestly is more useful than a borrowed standard applied loosely. Flag the real constraint politely — some mandates require specific alignment, which is fund documentation rather than a judgement on the issuer.

F. Cross-border and global Where your vantage point is a genuine asset

43
MediumCross-border

Is this a global market, or a European product with tourists?

Classic Kemmish framing — meet the wit, then be substantive. Today it's a European pricing market with global issuance: the deepest bid, the index architecture and the regulatory definitions are European, so a Canadian, Australian or Korean issuer is largely priced against European comparables. It becomes global when a non-European buyer base sets the price for non-European paper. "We'll know it's global when a Singapore deal doesn't need a Frankfurt investor to decide what it's worth."

44
HardCross-borderReputational risk

Basel III divergence between the US, EU and UK — what does fragmentation do to cross-border demand?

Don't criticise a named regulator or comment on any jurisdiction's political direction.

Stick to mechanics: divergent capital and liquidity treatment means the same bond is worth a different amount to buyers in different jurisdictions, so the marginal buyer becomes jurisdiction-specific and cross-border arbitrage weakens. Effect: more dispersion, thinner secondary in the non-domestic name, and a bigger premium for anything needing a cross-border buyer to clear. "Fragmentation doesn't usually show up as wider spreads. It shows up as fewer people in the book."

45
HardCross-borderReputational risk

Has geopolitics entered your country risk assessment in ways it hadn't five years ago?

Never describe HSBC's country risk appetite, limits or internal process. Industry generalities only, then stop.

"I can talk about how the market thinks, not how my institution decides." Generally: investors now ask about payment infrastructure, sanctions and convertibility as questions separate from credit — those determine whether you get paid rather than whether the borrower can pay. For a covered bond that's live, since the structure assumes an administrator can operate and remit. Then stop. Do not continue into examples.

46
MediumCross-border

Which Asian or non-European model travels best to new markets?

Compare structures rather than ranking countries: the agency/refinancing model (Korea, Malaysia, Japan) travels well into fragmented banking systems; the legislative bank-issuer model (Europe, Canada, Australia, Singapore) travels well where a handful of banks dominate origination and the insolvency regime is strong. "Match the model to your banking system's concentration, not to whichever conference you attended most recently."

47
MediumCross-border

What could Europe learn from non-European markets, rather than the other way round?

Generous, memorable, flatters your co-panellists. Three candidates: Canada's single registry and one supervisor for the whole product; Canada and Australia's discipline on issuance caps as a public encumbrance limit; and the agency markets' willingness to standardise loan documentation at origination, which is what actually makes a pool comparable. "Europe harmonised the bond. Some other markets harmonised the mortgage, which is harder and worth more."

48
MediumCross-borderInvestor lens

Can the eurozone become a net exporter of covered bonds — or only of covered bond standards?

Cross-reference Panel V and be precise: Europe already exports the standard (most new frameworks are recognisably EU-derived) and imports the issuance (non-EEA issuers raising euros). Net export of the product itself requires a non-European buyer base deep enough to absorb European paper — that's a savings and pension-system question, not a covered bond question.

"Around the world in 80 deals" — but is anyone actually asking for these bonds, or are we manufacturing supply to fill a conference agenda?

Enjoy it, answer straight. Demand for the asset class is structural and rules-driven, so it's real. Demand for any particular new name is not automatic and shouldn't be — that discipline is what keeps the label meaning something. "The market doesn't need eighty deals. It needs eight markets that are still here in 2035, and we don't yet know which eight."

Aren't you just talking your book?

Short, good-humoured, no defensiveness. "If I were, I'd be arguing for less supply and wider spreads, and I'm doing the opposite." Then the substance: a buy-and-hold liquidity investor's interest is in more eligible issuers, not fewer — which is genuinely aligned with everyone on this panel.

51
HardReputational risk

Which of these new markets is going to disappoint us? Name one.

Never name a country. Most likely to be quoted, most likely to cause an internal problem.

"I'll give you the failure mode rather than the country — partly because it's more useful and mostly because I'd like to be invited back." The pattern that disappoints: a framework passed to satisfy an external adviser, one debut done for the photograph, then a three-year gap. "It isn't bad markets that disappoint investors. It's good markets that stop issuing."

52
HardReputational riskStructure & reg

What's the systemic risk of exporting covered bonds to countries with untested insolvency regimes?

Take it seriously — it's the best question on the list. Two real risks: label contagion, where one bad outcome reprices an asset class built on the assumption that this never happens; and encumbrance, where a young banking system pledges its best assets before it has a deposit base or a resolution regime to absorb the rest. Mitigations exist and are public: issuance caps, supervisory approval, encumbrance disclosure. "The product isn't the risk. Doing it without the second half of the framework is." Note the moderator has publicly argued encumbrance concerns are overstated — expect pushback and welcome it.

HardReputational riskInvestor lens

Are covered bonds over-rated — literally? Should anyone believe a AAA from a frontier market?

Do not criticise rating agencies by name; four of them are speaking at this congress.

"A rating is an opinion about default probability. It was never a promise about liquidity, and liquidity is usually what an investor actually feels." So the AAA can be defensible and still not be the reason you own it. Redirect to your ground: "I don't buy the rating. I buy what the rating is derived from, and then I ask what happens if the derivation is wrong."

Absolute no-go. Positions, flows, limits, governance. No hedged version exists.

Getting from a statute to that state takes a decade and a first buyer willing to be early. "Safety is an outcome of institutional maturity, not an input."
