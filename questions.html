The set-level read

Forty-five minutes, six panellists, eleven questions. Richard gets through seven or eight at best. You are named in four: Q5, Q6, Q7 and Q11.

The running order works against you. Q1–Q4 are issuer show-and-tell — four sequential narratives that will eat over half the clock. Your named questions then cluster in the compressed back half. The realistic outcome is twenty quiet minutes followed by three questions in a rush.

The fix. Take one uninvited intervention early, on Q2 or Q3. Thirty seconds, no more. It plants you as the buy-side reference point so Richard starts coming back to you by instinct rather than by list.

Two questions walk into your guardrails. Q5 asks you for cost. Q7 asks whether you’d look at a Balkan debut. Both have clean answers below, and both are worth asking him to reword — the rewrites give him better material too.

The missing question. Nobody asks who actually buys a debut covered bond. That is the panel’s blind spot and your home ground. Offer it to him as an insert after Q5.

The file is OCR-damaged — “farniliar”, “transact credible”, “wouid you iook”, “W decisions”. Q8 has words missing outright. Ask for a clean copy; the lost text may hide a sharper question than the one you can see.

Your one device: the five gates

Use this on Q5, Q6, Q7, Q11 and every ambush. It answers “would you buy X” without ever giving a credit view, because it describes how institutional portfolios work rather than what you think of anyone.

  1. Eligibility — liquidity treatment, risk weighting, central bank repo. Binary, no partial credit.
  2. Index — inclusion, minimum size, rating floor. This is the benchmarked bid.
  3. Rating — achievable uplift against the country ceiling.
  4. Operations — country limits, currency, ICSD settlement, English documentation, listing, onboarding.
  5. Liquidity — free float, committed market-makers, repo-ability, a credible deal two and three.

Say this once, early — everything later is a callback Credit is gate zero, and it’s usually the easiest one. The four after it are where debuts actually die.

1. Murodjon — Uzbekistan’s first securitisation

Not yours — comment only if invited

The read-across, if he opens it up: a securitisation builds the plumbing a covered bond later needs — loan-level data, servicing records, a valuation convention, registrar, paying agent, trustee. Uzbekistan did it in a regulatory sandbox with five originating banks. That is market-building design rather than a funding trade, and it is the right sequence.

Facts you can drop. First placement 8 July 2026 on the Tashkent RSE, UZS 40.56bn, issued by UMRC SPV LLC as the opening tranche of a UZS 200bn pilot under Presidential Decree PD-26 of 21 February 2025. Originators: NBU, Asakabank, Uzpromstroybank, Xalq, Agrobank. The new capital markets bill runs to 16 chapters and 123 articles, drafted with EBRD, IFC, ADB, IsDB and IOSCO input.

Reword: four questions in one. The capital-markets-law part should be a follow-up.

2. Rada — Georgia, from repo collateral to real investors

Your early intervention

His premise checks out, so don’t correct it. Georgia passed its covered bond law in 2022 off the back of an EBRD–NBG technical assistance project begun in 2021, and covered bonds sit on the central bank’s eligible collateral list.

Your thirty seconds: an instrument designed for the central bank gets sized for the central bank. No free float, no price discovery, no secondary. It is the retained issuance problem in miniature — which Panel IV is debating two doors down at the same moment.

What the transition actually needs: free float and benchmark size; disclosure in English on a harmonised template; a currency with a functioning swap market; listed and ICSD-settled. Until those exist the third-party bid isn’t priced wide, it is simply absent.

The question isn’t what the bond looks like while the central bank is buying it. It’s what it looks like the day the central bank stops.

3. Kees — Triodos: smaller, newer, differently rated

Not yours — but a trap sits in the wording

“Less highly rated” invites a credit view on a named issuer sitting next to you. Do not take it. Speak about smaller issuers as a class throughout.

Your add: the differentiator isn’t roadshow effort, it’s an upfront decision about whether the programme can ever reach benchmark size and repeat. Investors will press on replenishment capacity, because a small pool ages faster relative to issuance than a large one. And a strong energy-efficiency profile buys a defined bid, not a broader one — green is a segment, not a substitute for liquidity.

Reword: ask him to drop “less highly rated” for “smaller and newer”, and let Kees raise the rating himself if he wants to.

4. Peter — Fairstone: Canada, currency, encumbrance

Not yours — check the premise before you speak

Fairstone doesn’t appear on CMHC’s registered programme list. If Peter is a prospective rather than actual issuer, the framing changes. Don’t assume a live programme.

OSFI caps assets pledged for covered bonds at 5.5% of on-balance-sheet assets — revised calculation effective 1 August 2019, with the temporary pandemic increase to 10% unwound in 2021. CMHC-insured mortgages and loans above 80% LTV are excluded from cover pools; substitute assets are capped at 10% of the pool.

Your point, and it is a good one: the cap is a percentage of total assets, so for a small bank the absolute number is small — and it collides with benchmark size. A modest balance sheet multiplied by 5.5% may not support a liquid curve at all. That is the real bind, and it is arithmetic rather than credit.

On currency, it cuts both ways. A foreign-currency deal gets a bigger book but adds swap costs and basis risk a small treasury may not be resourced to run. A domestic deal is cheaper to execute into a thin bid for a non-Big-Six name.

5. Elena and Aseem — cost implications

You are named

This is the price question. No number, in basis points or otherwise, including hypothetically. Give him something better than a number: give him where the cost sits.

  • Setup — legal, rating, cover pool monitor, systems, reporting. Fixed, doesn’t scale down. For a small programme it dominates everything else.
  • Debut concession — real, one-off, and the least interesting part of the answer.
  • The curve — what deals two and three clear at, driven by index status and liquidity rather than credit.

The concession on the debut is the cheapest thing about it. The expensive part is what deal three costs when nobody’s making a market in deals one and two.

Reword to ask for: “What are the cost implications?” becomes “Where does the cost actually show up?” Same question, invites structure instead of a number.

6. Aseem — new country versus new issuer

You are named, solo — your best question

New issuer in a known country: credit and size work inside a framework that already exists. The gates are open. Weeks.

New country: every gate is shut, and some of them don’t open by analysis. Eligibility is a policy determination. Index inclusion is a rules decision. A country limit is set by a risk framework, not a portfolio manager. Quarters, sometimes longer — and the approval sits with people who never see the deal.

A new issuer is a credit decision. A new country is an infrastructure decision — and infrastructure decisions don’t get made deal by deal.

The kicker, if you want the quote of the session Which is why the country that matters most in a debut is often not the issuer’s. It’s the investor’s.

7. The Balkans — “would you look at it?”

You are named — highest risk on the sheet

Split the verb first. “We look at everything. Looking is free.” Light, defuses, buys you the room.

Then run the gates as conditions. What would have to be true:

  • benchmark size in a currency with a functioning swap market
  • a segregation opinion that survives issuer insolvency under local law — an opinion, not an intention
  • at least one international rating with visible headroom to the country ceiling
  • harmonised transparency reporting, in English
  • listed and ICSD-settled
  • committed market-making beyond the syndicate desks

The honest observation, which is also the provocative one If the first deal only clears because a development bank takes half of it, you’ve financed a bank. You haven’t opened a market.

Don’t confirm which Balkan countries have passed laws. Montenegro, Albania and Moldova are unverified, and Rada owns that ground.

Don’t name a preferred country. Don’t say yes.

Reword to ask for: “Would you look at it?” becomes “What would have to be true before it reached your desk?”

8. Secondary regulations and execution risk

Not addressed to you — take it anyway

The strongest question on the sheet, and an investor question in disguise. What must be settled before deal one rather than learned through issuance:

  • insolvency segregation, and whether it is enforceable
  • who administers the cover pool after issuer default, and with what powers
  • maturity extension — the trigger, who pulls it, what it does to the swaps
  • valuation methodology and revaluation frequency for LTV
  • the reporting template, and its language
  • eligibility determinations obtained before pricing, not after

Flexibility left to the regulator is optionality written against the investor. And it doesn’t get priced wide. It just doesn’t get priced — we don’t show up.

9. Encumbrance limits versus market access

Open to all — read the room first

Richard has publicly argued encumbrance is “not so bad” and will fish for agreement. Don’t be a stooge and don’t be contrarian for sport. Take the buyer’s position — you hold both secured and unsecured bank paper, which nobody else on the panel can say.

The level matters less than three things: is the cap disclosed, is it stable, and is what’s left behind transparent. A predictable low cap is easier to underwrite than a generous discretionary one, because discretion gets exercised for someone else at the worst possible moment.

On persuading regulators, the argument isn’t “let us encumber more”. It is that a smaller bank with a tested secured funding channel depends less on its flightiest liabilities. That is a financial-stability case, and it needs evidence rather than advocacy — which the industry has not yet assembled.

Don’t lobby, speak for any institution’s regulatory view, or comment on a named regulator’s decision.

10. Routes to market

Open to all — take it

The investor’s answer reframes the question usefully: I don’t care who owns the process, I care that someone does, and that the output is standard enough to build a limit framework around.

Contract-law issuance is fast and produces a bond. A law is slow and produces a category. You can’t build a country limit around a bespoke structure; you can build one around a category.

Contract law gets you a bond. A law gets you a market.

On ownership: it should sit with whoever will still be there for deal five. Central-bank-led routes get the plumbing right and the demand wrong. Development-bank-led routes get deal one done and can leave the market dependent on the development bank.

11. Which debuts would you most like to see

You are named — this is your close

A preference isn’t a credit view, but naming a country reads as endorsement. Answer with a characteristic instead of a map reference.

I’d rather see the second deal from a market that has already debuted than a first deal from a new one. A debut proves a law works. The second and third prove a market exists — and that’s the one that gets me an index, a market-maker and a limit.

If pushed to name oneThe one I’d most like to see is the one that prices without a development bank in the book.
