## Your one device: the five gates

Use this on Q5, Q6, Q7, Q11 and every ambush. It answers "would you buy" without a credit view,
because it describes how institutional portfolios work, not what you think of anyone.

1. **Eligibility** — HQLA classification, risk weighting, central bank repo. Binary. No partial credit.
2. **Index** — inclusion, minimum size, rating floor. This is the benchmarked bid.
3. **Rating** — achievable uplift against the country ceiling.
4. **Operations** — country limits, currency, ICSD settlement, English documentation, listing, onboarding.
5. **Liquidity** — free float, committed market-makers, repo-ability, a credible deal two and three.

**The line:** *"Credit is gate zero, and it's usually the easiest one. The four after it are where
debuts actually die."*

Say this once, early. Then every later answer is a callback rather than a new argument.

## Question by question

### Q1 — Murodjon / Uzbekistan's first securitisation
**Not yours.** Comment only if invited.

**Your 20 seconds:** a securitisation builds the plumbing a covered bond later needs — loan-level
data, servicing records, a valuation convention, registrar, paying agent, trustee. Uzbekistan did
it in a regulatory sandbox with five originating banks. That's market-building design, not a
funding trade, and it's the right sequence.

**Facts you can use:** first placement 8 July 2026 on the Tashkent RSE, UZS 40.56bn, issued by
UMRC SPV LLC as the opening tranche of a UZS 200bn pilot under Presidential Decree PD-26
(21 Feb 2025). Originators: NBU, Asakabank, Uzpromstroybank, Xalq, Agrobank. The new capital
markets bill — 16 chapters, 123 articles — was drafted with EBRD, IFC, ADB, IsDB and IOSCO input.

**Reword:** four questions in one. Split the capital-markets-law part into a follow-up.

### Q2 — Rada / Georgia: repo collateral → real investors
**Premise checks out.** Georgia passed its covered bond law in 2022 off the back of an EBRD–NBG
technical assistance project started 2021, and covered bonds sit on the NBG's eligible collateral
list. Don't correct him.

**Your 30 seconds — this is your early intervention.** An instrument designed for the central bank
gets sized for the central bank. No free float, no price discovery, no secondary. It's the retained
issuance problem in miniature — which Panel IV is debating two doors down at the same time.

**What the transition needs:** free float and benchmark size; disclosure in English on a harmonised
template; a currency with a functioning swap market; listed and ICSD-settled. Until those, the
third-party bid isn't priced wide — it's simply absent.

**The line:** *"The question isn't what the bond looks like while the central bank is buying it.
It's what it looks like the day the central bank stops."*

### Q3 — Kees / Triodos: smaller, newer, differently rated
**Trap:** "less highly rated" invites a credit view on a named issuer sitting next to you. Do not
take it. Speak about *smaller issuers as a class* throughout.

**Your add:** the differentiator isn't roadshow effort. It's an upfront decision about whether the
programme can ever reach benchmark size *and repeat*. Investors will press on replenishment
capacity, because a small pool ages faster relative to issuance than a large one. And a strong
energy-efficiency profile buys a **defined** bid — not a broader one. Green is a segment, not a
substitute for liquidity.

**Reword:** ask Richard to drop "less highly rated" and use "smaller and newer". Let Kees decide
whether to raise the rating himself.

### Q4 — Peter / Fairstone: Canada, currency choice, encumbrance
**Premise risk — check before you speak.** Your notes flagged no public covered bond programme,
and Fairstone doesn't appear on CMHC's registered programme list. If Peter is a *prospective*
issuer, the question's framing changes and you shouldn't assume a live programme.

**Facts, confirmed:** OSFI caps assets pledged for covered bonds at **5.5% of on-balance-sheet
assets** (revised calculation effective 1 Aug 2019; the temporary COVID increase to 10% was
unwound in 2021). CMHC-insured mortgages and loans above 80% LTV are excluded from cover pools;
substitute assets capped at 10% of the pool.

**Your point, and it's a good one:** the cap is a percentage of total assets, so for a small bank
the absolute number is small — and it collides with benchmark size. Cap × modest balance sheet =
a programme that may never support a liquid curve. That's the real bind, and it's arithmetic, not
credit.

**On currency:** it cuts both ways. A foreign-currency deal gets a bigger book but adds swap costs
and basis risk a small treasury may not be resourced to run. A domestic deal is cheaper to execute
into a thin bid for a non-Big-Six name.

### Q5 — Elena + Aseem: cost implications
**This is the price question. Do not give a number, in basis points or otherwise, including
hypothetically.** Give him better than a number: give him where the cost sits.

**Three buckets:**
1. **Setup** — legal, rating, cover pool monitor, systems, reporting. Fixed, doesn't scale down.
   For a small programme it dominates everything else.
2. **Debut concession** — real, one-off, and the least interesting part of the answer.
3. **The curve** — what deals two and three clear at. Driven by index status and liquidity, not credit.

**The line:** *"The concession on the debut is the cheapest thing about it. The expensive part is
what deal three costs when nobody's making a market in deals one and two."*

**Reword request:** "What are the cost implications?" → **"Where does the cost actually show up?"**
Same question, invites structure instead of a number, better answer for the audience.

### Q6 — Aseem, solo: new country vs new issuer in a known country
**Your best question. Prepare this one properly.**

New issuer, known country: credit and size work inside a framework that already exists. The gates
are open. Weeks.

New country: every gate is shut, and some of them don't open by analysis. Eligibility is a policy
determination. Index inclusion is a rules decision. A country limit is set by a risk framework, not
a portfolio manager. Quarters, sometimes longer — and the approval sits with people who never see
the deal.

**The line:** *"A new issuer is a credit decision. A new country is an infrastructure decision —
and infrastructure decisions don't get made deal by deal."*

**The kicker, if you want the quote of the session:** *"Which is why the country that matters most
in a debut is often not the issuer's. It's the investor's."*

### Q7 — Balkans, and "Aseem, would you look at it?"
**Highest-risk question on the sheet.** Three moves, in order.

**One — split the verb.** "We look at everything. Looking is free." Light, defuses, buys the room.

**Two — run the gates as conditions.** What would have to be true:
- benchmark size in a currency with a functioning swap market
- a segregation opinion that survives issuer insolvency under local law — an opinion, not an intention
- at least one international rating with visible headroom to the country ceiling
- harmonised transparency reporting, in English
- listed, ICSD-settled
- committed market-making beyond the syndicate desks

**Three — the honest observation, which is also the provocative one:**
*"If the first deal only clears because a development bank takes half of it, you've financed a
bank. You haven't opened a market."*

**Do not:** confirm which Balkan countries have passed laws — I couldn't verify Montenegro, Albania
or Moldova, and Rada owns that ground. Don't name a preferred country. Don't say yes.

**Reword request:** "Aseem, would you look at it?" → **"Aseem, what would have to be true before it
reached your desk?"** Better for you, better answer for the room.

### Q8 — secondary regulations and execution risk
**Not addressed to you. Take it anyway** — it's the strongest question on the sheet and it's an
investor question in disguise.

**What must be settled before deal one, not learned through issuance:**
- insolvency segregation and its enforceability
- who administers the cover pool after issuer default, and with what powers
- maturity extension: the trigger, who pulls it, what it does to the swaps
- valuation methodology and revaluation frequency for LTV
- the reporting template, and its language
- eligibility determinations — repo, liquidity treatment — obtained *before* pricing, not after

**The line:** *"Flexibility left to the regulator is optionality written against the investor. And
it doesn't get priced wide. It just doesn't get priced — we don't show up."*

**Reword:** supply Richard with a clean version; his text is missing words.

### Q9 — encumbrance limits vs market access
**Read the room first.** Richard has publicly argued encumbrance is "not so bad" and will fish for
agreement. Don't be a stooge and don't be contrarian for sport. Take the buyer's position — you
hold both secured and unsecured bank paper, which nobody else on the panel can say.

**Your position:** the level matters less than three things — is the cap disclosed, is it stable,
and is what's left behind transparent. A predictable low cap is easier to underwrite than a
generous discretionary one, because discretion gets exercised for someone else at the worst
possible moment.

**On persuading regulators:** the argument isn't "let us encumber more". It's that a smaller bank
with a tested secured funding channel depends less on its flightiest liabilities. That's a
financial-stability case, and it needs evidence rather than advocacy — which the industry has not
yet assembled.

**Do not:** lobby, speak for any institution's regulatory view, or comment on a named regulator's
decision.

### Q10 — routes to market: central bank, contract law, MDB, industry-led
**Take it.** The investor's answer reframes the question usefully.

I don't care who owns the process. I care that *someone* does, and that the output is standard
enough to build a limit framework around.

**The distinction that matters:** contract-law issuance is fast and produces a bond. A law is slow
and produces a category. You can't build a country limit around a bespoke structure. You can build
one around a category.

**The line:** *"Contract law gets you a bond. A law gets you a market."*

**On ownership:** it should sit with whoever will still be there for deal five. Central-bank-led
routes get the plumbing right and the demand wrong. MDB-led routes get deal one done and can leave
the market dependent on the MDB.

### Q11 — which debuts do you expect / which would you most like to see
**Your closing line. Make it count.** A preference isn't a credit view, but naming a country reads
as endorsement — so answer with a characteristic, not a map reference.

**The close:**
*"I'd rather see the second deal from a market that has already debuted than a first deal from a
new one. A debut proves a law works. The second and third prove a market exists — and that's the
one that gets me an index, a market-maker and a limit."*

**If pushed to name:** *"The one I'd most like to see is the one that prices without a development
bank in the book."*

## Ambush drills

| They say | You say |
|---|---|
| "Give me a number." | "I'll give you the components. The number is someone else's job." |
| "Would you buy Uzbekistan?" | "That's an eligibility question before it's a credit question." |
| "What's your book doing?" | Decline in one clause, then pivot: "— but here's what any treasury book runs into." |
| Anything on HSBC funding / a named sovereign | One short decline. Move on. Don't explain the refusal twice. |

Rule: refuse **once**, briefly, then immediately give them something better. A long refusal sounds
evasive. A short refusal followed by a good point sounds disciplined.
