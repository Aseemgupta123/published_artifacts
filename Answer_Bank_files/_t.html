Anchor line — the one thing to land

The interesting thing about a first securitisation is not the bond. It is the plumbing it forces you to build.

How to answer

  • Wait to be invited. This is Murodjon's to answer. Come in only if Richard turns to you, or at the very end with one sentence.
  • Your angle is sequencing. Securitisation before covered bonds is the right order — it forces loan-level data standardisation, tested legal transfer, servicing and back-up servicing, and independent verification. Those are all preconditions for a covered bond.
  • Precedent matters more than size. The first question a cross-border investor asks about a new market is whether anything has ever actually been done. A completed transaction changes that conversation.
  • Frame the capital-markets law as the second half. A law without a demonstrated transaction is theory; a transaction without a law is a one-off. You need both.

Guardrails — do not go here

  • Do not say whether you would buy Uzbek paper, or comment on Uzbekistan's rating, sovereign credit or reform trajectory.
  • Do not comment on the ADB's or any MDB's role in the transaction beyond what Murodjon has said publicly on the panel.
  • Do not congratulate in a way that sounds like endorsement — "an important milestone for the market" is fine, "an attractive opportunity" is not.
  • Avoid speculating on pricing or demand for a future Uzbek covered bond.

Open on the plumbing · then sequencing · then what travels internationally · close on the law

From where I sit, the interesting thing about a first securitisation is not the bond — it is the plumbing it forces you to build. To get a transaction away you have to standardise loan-level data, prove the legal transfer actually works, appoint a trustee, document servicing and back-up servicing, and have someone independent verify the pool. Every one of those is also a precondition for a covered bond.

So the sequencing is sensible. Securitisation first, because it is the harder discipline — the investor is taking the assets and nothing else, so the assets have to be properly described. Covered bonds second, because by then the data standards and the legal machinery already exist and you are adding recourse on top of something that has been tested rather than building both at once.

The second thing a first transaction does is create a precedent. When an international investor looks at a market they have never held, the first question is not about spread. It is whether anything has ever actually been done — whether a transfer has been executed, whether a trustee has ever had to act, whether the loan data has survived contact with a third party. A completed transaction changes that conversation from a hypothetical to a track record.

What I would say from the buy-side, though, is that one deal is a data point and a pipeline is a market. The work an investor has to do on a new jurisdiction is essentially a fixed cost — the legal framework review, the credit approval, the operational set-up. That cost is much easier to justify if you can see a programme behind it rather than a single transaction.

On the capital-markets law: I would think of the law and the transaction as two halves of the same thing. A law without a demonstrated transaction is theory, and international investors are appropriately sceptical of theory. A transaction without a durable legal framework is a one-off that does not scale. Having done the deal first means that when the law arrives, the market can say: here is the statute, and separately, here is the proof that the operational and legal steps behind it work in practice. That is a much stronger position than most new markets start from.

Aseem, would you buy an Uzbek covered bond?

The trap

A yes is an investment view on a sovereign and on a co-panellist's institution. A flat no is discourteous and makes the rest of the session awkward.

Your line

I would read the framework — that part is genuinely free, and I would encourage any issuer in a new market to make it easy to read. What I can't do from this chair is give you a view on any particular name. What I can tell you is what would put it in front of me: a legal opinion that addresses insolvency rather than just validity, transparency to a recognised standard, and a pipeline with more than one transaction in it.

How long would it take you to get comfortable with a new jurisdiction?

The trap

Implies an internal process timeline and could be read as describing the bank's credit approval machinery.

Your line

I'd rather answer that in terms of what drives it than in weeks. The gating item is almost never analyst time — it's getting a clean legal opinion on what happens to the cover pool in issuer insolvency, and getting the pool data in a form that can be modelled. Issuers who prepare those two things early shorten the process more than anything else they can do.
